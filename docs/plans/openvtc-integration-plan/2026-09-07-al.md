# 2026-09-07 — Alberto

Review of [`vta-carriage_subtask.md`](./vta-carriage_subtask.md) at
`docs/vta-to-vta-carriage-plan` (keyring-wallet
[#19](https://github.com/berkmancenter/keyring-wallet/pull/19), head `f02ae8e`),
read as part of the pre-Prague branch consolidation. The design holds — the
three-axis split is right and the plan is a legitimate rung on top of the Trust
Task recast rather than a parallel track. Five things it does not say, and one
demo shape it implies but never names.

Nothing here reverses a position in the plan. Four of the five are blanks, not
disagreements: the plan is silent where it needs to choose, and each blank is
load-bearing for the phase it sits in.

## Finding 1 — the plan never says *which* mediator

`mediator` appears three times (§2's topology table, §5's `handoff` member,
§6's async-delay argument), always as a generic DIDComm queue. That is fine as
far as the topology argument goes, and it is not fine as an implementation
input.

Two mediators are in play and the plan does not pick one:

- **Keyring's own Credo mediator** — `credo-mediator.asml.berkmancenter.org`,
  the one every exchange uses today and the one whose pickup behaviour
  [`2026-08-24-bam.md`](./2026-08-24-bam.md) Part I root-caused.
- **The VTI stack's own mediator**, which the `verifiable-trust-infrastructure`
  deployment provides.

A VTA-mediated `propose` crosses whichever one carries it, so the choice
decides whose queue holds a cold contact's first message, whose operator can
observe it, and which codebase a delivery bug lands in. §7's table of upstream
asks is written against `vta-service` and `openvtc` without stating what sits
between them and us.

**The plan edit this implies:** §2's topology table gains a row, or §6 a
sentence, naming the mediator per mode — and saying explicitly if the answer is
"the VTI stack's, once the VTA is the counterparty."

## Finding 2 — `did:webvh` resolution is absent, and it is on the critical path

The document contains **no occurrence** of `webvh`, `did:webvh` or `didwebvh`.
§5's delegate-service entry has to be resolved from somewhere, and §4's
discovery ladder is a resolution story end to end; both are written as though a
resolver is a given.

It is not a given, and we have already measured that it is not:

- [`ref-06x-cypress-stack`](../../tsp-reference/ref-06x-cypress-stack/) measured
  **Credo 0.6.3 returning `unsupportedDidMethod` for `did:webvh`**, and proved a
  ~20-line wallet-side resolver adapter sufficient.
- That adapter is already **parent Phase D item 3**, and
  [`pnm_cnm_subtask.md`](./pnm_cnm_subtask.md) marks it *"shared with parent
  Phase D item 3."*
- [`keyring-on-the-vta-farm.md`](../keyring-on-the-vta-farm.md) §3.4 states the
  same dependency from the Farm side, and is careful that the need predates the
  Farm rather than being created by it.

So the work is tracked — in two sibling documents, and not in this one. This
plan should **cite** the shared item rather than re-open it, and say which of
its own phases is blocked without it. On the evidence above that is every phase
that resolves a VTA delegate entry, which is §4 and §5 entire.

**Worth noting for whoever writes that edit:** `@credo-ts/webvh` **is** already
a dependency in `app/package.json` at `0.6.3`. Its presence in the manifest is
not evidence the method resolves — `ref-06x` measured the failure with it
installed. Anyone reading the dependency list alone would conclude the opposite.

## Finding 3 — the policy surface is specified as a CLI, and Keyring has no CLI

§5 and §7 state the authorization surface in `pnm-cli` terms:
`pnm relationships policy set --default {accept,reject,consent}`,
`pnm relationships policy explain`. §9 already makes the sharper structural
observation — that *"neither `pnm-cli` nor (so far as found) `pnm-core` has any
relationship code at all"*, so this is new surface rather than an extension.

What §9 does not draw out is the consequence for us: **a policy the user sets
from a phone is not the same product as a flag they pass to a binary.** A CLI
default is set once by someone who has read the help text. A wallet default is
set by someone who will be asked, at some later moment, to trust the answer.
The three dispositions are the same three; the affordance, the wording, and the
moment of asking are ours to design and are not designed anywhere yet.

This is the same asymmetry that runs through
[`keyring-on-the-vta-farm.md`](../keyring-on-the-vta-farm.md)'s L0–L3 table,
where each level's upstream step is a CLI action and the Keyring column is a
different interaction rather than a translation of it. This plan should say so
in §9 rather than inherit the CLI framing by default.

## Finding 4 — nothing says how a VTA gets attached to the wallet in the first place

§9 lists four Keyring-side changes: the discovery ladder, `propose`/`#response`
handling, handoff consumption, and a cross-reference into P4. All four assume a
VTA is *already* associated with the user.

There is no step anywhere in the plan where that association is made. Before
the delegate-resolution path in §4 can find anything, some flow has to exist in
which a person binds a VTA to their wallet — in onboarding, in settings, or as
a deliberate later action.

That flow is also the first place Keyring meets the Farm: the L0 row of
[`keyring-on-the-vta-farm.md`](../keyring-on-the-vta-farm.md) is *"the phone
displays its temp `did:key`; the human pastes it into the tab already open"* —
which is precisely a VTA-onboarding interaction, described in a sibling plan
and owned by neither. It should be owned by one of them explicitly.

## Finding 5 — the seven open questions are not equally open

§11 marks Q1 (does a cold `propose` need an out-of-band pairing step, or is a
correctly-proofed `propose` from an unknown DID enough) as *"a real fork, not a
settled default"*, blocking §5's shape. Agreed that it is a fork. Adding the
consideration that makes it decidable:

Findings 3 and 4 both land on the same surface Q1 does. If the wallet needs a
VTA-attachment flow anyway (Finding 4), and needs its own policy affordance
anyway (Finding 3), then the OOB-first design's *"UX step this plan has not
designed"* is substantially a step we are designing regardless. That does not
settle Q1 — the trust models genuinely differ — but it removes the cost
asymmetry the recommendation currently leans on.

Q3 (default disposition: accept/reject/consent) is called a product decision
and marked as blocking nothing. That is right on the code path and wrong on the
demo path — see below.

## The demo this implies, which the plan never names

The plan's own mechanism produces a demonstration more legible than anything in
the current VRC exchange, and §10's phasing does not mention it.

**A proposal arrives and is accepted while the recipient's phone stays in their
pocket.** That is not a new capability to build — it is §5's PDP with
`--default accept` and the §3 handoff, exactly as specified.

The shape worth rehearsing is the **contrast**, not the auto-accept alone:

| | Party B — policy `accept` | Party C — policy `consent` |
|---|---|---|
| Phone | never leaves the pocket | buzzes, human decides |
| Who answered | B's always-on VTA, per saved policy | B's human, in the moment |
| Wire | identical `propose` → `#response` | identical `propose` → `#response` |

Auto-acceptance shown by itself is invisible — an audience sees nothing happen
and has to be told something did. Shown next to a phone that *does* buzz, the
policy becomes the visible object, which is the actual claim: the same protocol,
two dispositions, chosen by the recipient in advance.

**This makes Q3 demo-blocking even though it is not code-blocking.** The
contrast only reads if both dispositions are reachable and their defaults are
deliberate. It also exercises Q1 directly: whether the proposing party is a
cold contact or a pre-paired one is the difference between a demo that proves
the cold-contact path and one that quietly assumes it away.

**Not yet designed, and needed before this is rehearsable:** what Party B sees
*afterwards* — the record that their VTA accepted something on their behalf
while they were not looking. §5's Audit subsection covers the VTA side. The
wallet side of that story is unwritten, and it is the part an audience will ask
about first.
