# The applicant proves at the VTA, not on the phone — IN-264

**2026-10-14, AL.** Decisions and findings from reading upstream's hidden-vetting
inputs for IN-264 (the 8-step spec and the "Keyring Hidden Vetting Guide", both
written 14 October 2026 against vta-service 0.60, vta-sdk 0.68 and
trust-tasks-rs 0.31) against `feat/hidden-vetting-mode` as built.

## 1. Decision: the applicant's proof is built by the user's VTA

**Adopted, by Alberto, knowing what it costs.** Upstream's design has the
applicant's VTA derive a per-application PCS key, hold it, and build the proof;
Keyring carries messages between the VTA, the vetters and the community. The
guide is unambiguous: *"The app runs no PCS cryptography: your user's VTA holds
the keys and builds the proof, and Keyring carries messages between the VTA, the
vetters and the community."* On what the app may keep it lists, under **Never
holds**, *"A PCS key (`usk`): the VTA re-derives it on each call and never
returns it."*

**This supersedes the parent plan's §3.1/D4 answer for the applicant** — that the
phone proves — and with it the parts of
[`keyring_implementation_subtask.md`](./keyring_implementation_subtask.md) that
build a phone-side prover: the native module (§2), the secret's storage and the
biometric gate (§3). The vetter side is untouched by this decision and remains
as that document and [`vetter_role_subtask.md`](./vetter_role_subtask.md) have
it.

### What the decision does and does not cost

**It is not a move from hardware custody to a server.** The parent's §3.1 item 1
already establishes that every ZK secret here is a scalar on a pairing-friendly
curve and that *"neither phone secure element supports those curves"*, so a
device-held PCS key would have been a software key in the phone's store either
way. The change is from software-on-the-device to the user's own agent, not from
Secure Enclave to someone else's server. An earlier framing of mine, relayed
through the coordinator, called it "moving a key onto a server" without that
qualification; it was true but coarser than the plan's own analysis, and the
precise version is the one above.

What it does cost: a key that proves something about the user now lives
somewhere the user's phone cannot reach or audit directly, and its availability
is the agent's availability. That is the trade accepted in exchange for
interoperating with the ecosystem's only implementation.

## 2. The device prover was feasible; that is not why it is being dropped

The rung ladder `ref-24-pcs-host` … `ref-32-pcs-android` measured proving on
real hardware: 7.8 ms on an iPhone 16, 87.2 ms on a Galaxy A03s. Nothing about
the cryptography failed on a phone, and the measurements stand.

It is dropped because the community side and the VTA are built around the VTA
holding the key, and a second, device-side construction would have to be
accepted by `vta/pcs/verify-attestation` and by the community's verifier to be
worth anything. `react-native-pcs` is therefore **parked, not deleted**: the
vetter role may still want it, and deleting it would discard measured evidence
that the device path works.

## 3. No Rust is needed for the applicant flow

Upstream's prerequisites say to *"rebuild `vta-mobile-core` from current VTI
main"* for `build_pcs_task`/`parse_pcs_response`. That prerequisite is written
for a consumer without our machinery. Keyring already builds and signs `vta/*`
Trust Tasks in TypeScript — `VtaClient.ts` carries `vta/contexts/list/1.0`,
`vta/webvh/dids/create/1.0`, `rotate-keys`, `servers/list`, signing with
`signDocumentProof`/`signCompactJws` — so the five applicant tasks are five more
task types in that client, not a cross-compiled dependency.

**The trade:** their builder validates each payload against the task's generated
spec type, so a malformed request fails before signing; ours does not unless we
mirror the schemas. The design mirrors them in TypeScript with tests against the
registry, which is cheaper than a Rust toolchain in the mobile build and keeps
the failure mode (a schema test) visible in CI.

## 3.1 The generated types exist, and we are not taking them yet

The registry generates TypeScript payload types for all twelve PCS tasks
(`trust-tasks-ts/src/vta/pcs/*/0.1/payload.ts`), and importing them would be
better than mirroring schemas by hand — the app already imports generated types
of that kind. They are published in `@openvtc/trust-tasks` **0.26.1**; this
repository is pinned at **0.9.0**, and `scripts/openvtc/PINS.json` marks that
pin deliberately: *"the Cypress lock; any bump past 0.9.x = post-Cypress
framework movement"*. The installed 0.9.0 has no `vta/pcs` directory, so the
types cannot be had without the bump.

**Decision (coordinator, 2026-10-14): mirror the five applicant payloads by
hand and stay on 0.9.0.** Their tests validate the mirrored shapes against the
registry's own `payload.schema.json` files out of `external/`, so upstream's
source of truth is what grades us even though the dependency has not moved. The
0.9 → 0.26 bump is queued separately, with its own testing pass, after 242's
hidden vetting: seventeen minor versions across every Trust Task the app speaks
is not something hidden vetting should carry in as a side effect.

## 4. The agent is never asked anything until the user asks for something

To show *why* a hidden criterion is unusable rather than a bare "no", the app
needs the agent's version and the identity's capabilities. Both are knowable:
the capability from `VtaClient.listAcl()` (entries carry
`capabilities?: string[]`), the version from a side-effect-free
`vta/pcs/applications/show` with an unknown id — `notFound` means supported,
`unsupportedType` means pre-0.60.

**Neither may be asked before the user acts.** There is no read-only exemption
from the agent's approval rules: `vta-service/src/trust_tasks/policy_gate.rs`,
read at `vta-service-v0.60.0`, states *"Every dispatched Trust Task routes
through [`policy_gate`] before its handler runs"*, and a rule may answer
`requireConsent` or `requireStepUp`. So either probe can be gated.

**And a gated task wakes an approver by itself.** The VTA does not merely hand
the consent requests back to the caller: `trust_tasks/consent_request.rs` at
that tag documents the *"task-consent push hop to the mediator"*
(`CONSENT_PUSH_DELIVER_BY_SECS = 300`), noting that *"the mediator holds a
hop-accepted push for the device to collect whenever it next connects"*, and
`trust_tasks/helpers.rs` describes the annex as carrying one signed request per
approver *"so a requester can relay them where the VTA has no route"* — the
relay is the fallback, the push is the normal path.

**So the rule is: resolve the reason on tap.** The join card names the criterion
as private vetting with no reason attached; nothing is asked of the agent until
the user taps to join, at which point a prompt is legitimate because they just
asked for something. Decided with the coordinator, 2026-10-14.

### Superseded, earlier the same day

This document first concluded that up-front probing was safe provided Keyring
never routed the consent annex, on the strength of `helpers.rs` alone — the
annex is handed to the caller, so a prompt seemed to require the caller to act.
That is wrong in its conclusion: it accounts for what *our* app does and not for
what the VTA does, and the push hop above is what it missed. Recorded rather
than deleted because the mistake is an easy one to repeat: the annex really is
caller-routed, and reading only that file makes caller-routing look like the
whole mechanism.

The cost of the correction is small — the reason appears one round trip after
the tap instead of before it — and the harm it avoids is not: a join list
rendering could otherwise have woken an approver, who may be a different person
from the user, for a label.

## 5. What `feat/hidden-vetting-mode` keeps

Reading the branch against the eight steps, these already match and survive the
redesign: spotting a hidden criterion and failing closed on an unsupported
critical namespace (step 1), the `vetting/request` carrying
`ext["org.openvtc.hidden-vetting"]` (step 3), `vtc/vetting/pcs-challenge/0.1`
(step 5), and the applicant screen with its progress surface.

What changes: `hiddenVetting.ts` calls a native `prove`; that becomes
`vta/pcs/prove`. Attestations are verified locally; they go to
`vta/pcs/verify-attestation` instead and are explicitly **not** stored as
credentials.

## 6. Blocked, and on whom

- **End-to-end testing of the applicant flow** is blocked on openvtc **#487**,
  not only the vetter role. The VTA refuses the old attestation shape as
  `attestationInvalid`, so without a conforming vetter `prove` returns
  `noUsableAttestation` and the flow cannot complete. A question to upstream
  asking when #487 ships is drafted and waiting on Alberto's approval to post.
- **The test-vtc persona check** (a write against a live community) is held
  until the coordinator says go.
- **The three "why not" strings** for the narrowed `vettingPrivate`
  (`suiteUnsupported`, `agentTooOld`, `phoneNotAllowed`) are owned by UI/UX,
  confirmed 2026-10-14. This plan supplies the conditions, not the wording.
